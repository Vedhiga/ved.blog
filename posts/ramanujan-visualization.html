A Visualization Inspired by Ramanujan

Draft — May 2025 · 6 min read · by Dhruv DRAFT

Srinivasa Ramanujan had almost no formal training. He grew up in Kumbakonam — not so far from where I am now — and filled notebooks with identities that professional mathematicians couldn't prove for decades. He said his formulas came to him in dreams, from the goddess Namagiri.

Whether divine or not, his mathematics is undeniably beautiful. This post is my attempt to see some of it — to turn formulas into pictures.

The Taxicab Number — 1729

When Hardy visited a sick Ramanujan in hospital and mentioned he'd arrived in taxi number 1729, Ramanujan immediately replied: "That is a very interesting number — it is the smallest number expressible as the sum of two cubes in two different ways."

1729 = 1³ + 12³ = 9³ + 10³

This wasn't a party trick. It was a symptom of a mind that lived inside numbers the way most of us live inside language. The visualization below explores taxicab-style numbers — integers with multiple cube-sum representations — plotted as a grid.

Ramanujan's Infinite Series for π

One of his most stunning results was an infinite series that converges to 1/π with remarkable speed — each term adds roughly 8 correct decimal places of π:

1/π = (2√2 / 9801) · Σ [(4k)! · (1103 + 26390k)] / [(k!)⁴ · 396⁴ᵏ]

This looks like it was conjured from thin air. And yet it works — Gosper used it to compute π to 17 million digits in 1985. It later became the basis of the Chudnovsky algorithm used to compute trillions of digits today.

My Reflection

As someone from the same cultural soil — Tamil Nadu, devotion, the feeling that mathematics is something discovered rather than invented — Ramanujan resonates deeply. He saw the sacred in numbers. I think that's not mysticism. I think it's a very precise observation that the universe has arithmetic structure all the way down.

"An equation has no meaning unless it expresses a thought of God." — Srinivasa Ramanujan
